The concept

Two linked ideas: skills built from real corrections, and evals built from real taste.

Every's editor-in-chief has an eye for copy edits that cannot scale with the company. Rather than describe her taste in words, they took about 30,000 of her past edits and distilled them into a skill. Now anyone can ask for a "Kate pass" on a draft, and the agent leaves suggested changes in the Google Doc as her, which she accepts or rejects. The key move is the loop: they track the extra work she still adds after the agent's pass and compound it back into the skill. She sees a graph of time saved and suggested new rules. Taste is hard to describe in one go but easy to distill from a thousand examples.

The second idea is personal evals. Whenever someone likes or dislikes a model's output, they capture it and say why, and the platform turns that into unit-test-like checks (for example, "is the headline sentence case?"). They then run many models on the same real scenario and score each against their own taste. They contrast this with benchmarks the way you would contrast an SAT score with a reference check and a work trial: once every candidate scores 1600, you need to see how they do on your work.

Supporting ideas: one shared agent beats one agent per person, because gains compound when many people invest in the same skills. And the agent acts as a separate entity that, for tool calls, acts on behalf of whoever asked.

What's new vs familiar: learning from examples, writing evals and closing the loop are familiar patterns. What's distinctive is applying them to non-coding knowledge work, and treating the agent like a new hire who should be better on day 10 than day one. Despite the title, security gets only a short treatment here (isolation of sandboxes and memories, and acting on behalf of the requesting user); these notes focus on the compounding-skill idea.

What you could do with it

Kept general because it depends on which of your tasks has the biggest pile of past corrections.

New skill

Distill taste from my own historical corrections

Take any repeatable task where you've already corrected output many times (edits, rewrites, reviews) and package those corrections into a skill that applies the same judgment on request. Kate Pass works because it was built from tens of thousands of real edits, not a written description of taste.

Implementation plan

A correction-capture loop for an existing skill

For a skill you already use, log what you change in its output each time, and periodically fold those changes back into its rules. This is Every's "track the extra work she added, then compound it back" step, and it can be added without redesigning the skill. A simple version is a dated corrections file plus a periodic review that proposes new rules for approval.

Implementation plan

Personal evals from captured likes and dislikes

Keep a running list of outputs you liked or disliked with one line of why, convert recurring reasons into pass/fail checks, and run a skill or model against fixed scenarios. This gives you an eval that reflects your taste rather than a public benchmark, and makes a model or prompt change testable instead of a vibe check.

Prompt
You are helping me turn my own accumulated judgment into a self-improving skill. Context: a team built an AI coworker where one person's taste (an editor's ~30,000 historical copy edits) was distilled into a skill anyone could invoke. They then tracked every extra edit the editor still made after the agent's pass and folded it back into the skill so it improved over time. They also built personal evals: captured outputs they liked or disliked, wrote down why, and converted the reasons into unit-test-style checks (for example, "is the headline in sentence case?") to compare models on real work.

Do this with me, one step at a time, asking before you assume anything:
1. Ask me which task I repeat most where I routinely correct the output, and where my past corrections live (files, docs, git history, chat).
2. Read a sample of those corrections and propose a short list of the rules and tastes they imply. Show me concrete before/after examples for each rule, and ask me to confirm, reject or refine them.
3. Draft a skill (a SKILL.md with a clear trigger description) that applies the confirmed rules, plus a `corrections.md` log where each run's leftover manual edits get recorded.
4. Turn the confirmed rules into 10-20 pass/fail checks and a small set of fixed test scenarios, so I can re-run them whenever I change the skill or the model.
5. Propose a lightweight review routine, such as a weekly pass over `corrections.md` that suggests new rules for me to approve, so the skill compounds instead of going stale.

Keep everything in plain files I can version-control. Don't invent rules I haven't confirmed.
